1. Information search, evaluation and management

Build the judgement and practical skills needed to find, assess, organise and use digital information effectively in Queensland Government work.

Official DigComp 3.0 area description

To articulate information needs, and to search for, locate and retrieve digital information and content. To judge the relevance of the source and its content in digital environments. To critically evaluate digital sources, content, and processes used to generate them. To store, manage, organise and analyse digital information and data.

Why this area matters

Queensland public sector employees routinely use digital information to prepare advice, design services, administer programs, manage projects and make decisions.

Strong capability in this area helps employees find authoritative information, recognise unreliable or AI-generated content, and manage government information so it remains secure, discoverable and useful.

Using workplace tools

Tool availability and approval differ between agencies.

Use only systems approved by your agency and follow applicable information security, privacy, recordkeeping and AI requirements.

1.1 Browsing, searching and filtering information

What it is

Articulating what information you need, knowing where and how to search for it, accessing and navigating information in digital environments, and using search tools and filters to identify relevant information. DigComp 3.0 also highlights that search results depend on the search tool used and how the search is framed, including AI-driven and traditional search tools.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

What information do I need, where should I look, and how do I narrow the results?

rather than simply:

Can I Google it?

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Queensland Government employees regularly need to find policies, standards, datasets, legislation, guidance, previous project material, stakeholder information and operational records.

Strong search and filtering skills help employees:

  • Find authoritative information more quickly
  • Reduce time spent searching across disconnected systems
  • Avoid relying on outdated or irrelevant material
  • Use the right source for the task
  • Improve evidence-based advice and decision-making
  • Locate relevant government guidance, data and resources
  • Reduce duplication by finding existing work before creating something new
  • Navigate search results from traditional search tools and AI-supported tools more critically

ForGov, Queensland Government websites, the Open Data Portal and Microsoft 365 environments all contain useful information, but they work differently. Effective searching means choosing the right place to search, using useful search terms and filtering results to find what is relevant.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Search ForGov for a policy, directive, standard or guideline
  • Locate Queensland Government information through qld.gov.au or agency websites
  • Search the Open Data Portal for a relevant dataset
  • Find a previous project document in SharePoint or Teams
  • Search Outlook for an email, attachment or decision trail
  • Use Microsoft Search to locate a file, person, site or answer across Microsoft 365
  • Use search filters such as date, author, file type, site or category
  • Search for current legislation, standards or reporting requirements
  • Compare search results to identify the most relevant source
  • Refine search terms when the first results are not useful
  • Use AI-supported search or an approved AI tool to explore possible sources, then verify results through authoritative systems
Tools and processes

Tool availability, configuration and approval differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the digital systems and search environments used to find information, data and digital content.

  • ForGov search
  • qld.gov.au search
  • Queensland Government Open Data Portal
  • SharePoint search
  • Microsoft Search
  • Teams search
  • Outlook search
  • Agency intranets and knowledge bases
  • Approved records and document management systems
  • Microsoft Learn search
  • Approved AI tools or digital assistants, where authorised

Common processes

Processes are the search strategies and habits that help you find relevant information efficiently.

  • Defining the information need before searching
  • Choosing the most appropriate place to search
  • Using specific keywords and phrases
  • Trying alternative terms or plain-language variations
  • Applying filters such as date, file type, owner, source or category
  • Checking whether results are current and authoritative
  • Distinguishing relevant from irrelevant results
  • Saving or recording useful sources for future reference
  • Updating searches when new information becomes available
  • Verifying AI-generated search suggestions against authoritative sources
Try it at work
  1. Choose a current work question that requires information.
  2. Write the information need in one sentence.
  3. Identify three places you could search, such as ForGov, SharePoint, the Open Data Portal or Microsoft Search.
  4. Create three search terms or phrases.
  5. Run the search in at least two different places.
  6. Apply filters where available, such as date, file type, source, category or author.
  7. Compare the results and identify which are most relevant.
  8. Record which search terms and filters worked best.
  9. Identify whether you found an authoritative source or need to keep searching.

Hint: Good searching is not about finding the most results. It is about finding the most relevant and reliable results for your purpose.

DigComp 3.0-aligned proficiency guide

DigComp 3.0 describes eight proficiency levels grouped into four broad bands. For simplicity, this Hub presents the four bands: Basic, Intermediate, Advanced and Highly advanced. The descriptions below are practical workplace interpretations of DigComp 3.0 and are not verbatim reproductions of the official competence statements.

Basic

With guidance as needed, individuals can:

  • Acknowledge the benefits of using different digital search tools and methods depending on the purpose.
  • Identify the main features of commonly used AI-driven and traditional search tools.
  • Recognise that digital search results can include irrelevant information and depend on the search tool and search wording.
  • Use digital search tools to perform, refine and update basic information searches.

Intermediate

  • Purposefully explore new digital search tools and strategies.
  • Select appropriate digital search tools based on information needs.
  • Distinguish between more and less relevant digital search results or outputs.
  • Apply search strategies and filters to improve the relevance of results.
  • Adapt searches when initial results are not useful or complete.

Advanced

  • Organise complex search activities across multiple digital environments.
  • Select, combine and refine search methods for different information needs.
  • Evaluate whether search results are relevant, current and useful for the task.
  • Support others to improve search strategies.
  • Use search tools and digital sources efficiently across different work contexts.

Highly advanced

  • Develop or improve search strategies for complex or specialised information needs.
  • Guide others in using advanced search approaches and tools.
  • Contribute to better information discovery practices across a team or work area.
  • Identify gaps in searchability, metadata or information architecture that make information difficult to find.
  • Support improvements that help others find reliable information more easily.

Search Strategy Challenge

Objective

Define a work information need, search in more than one place, and identify the most useful and reliable result.

Learning outcomes

After completing this activity, you will be able to:

  • Define an information need clearly.
  • Choose suitable places and search terms.
  • Use filters to improve results.
  • Compare results and identify an authoritative source.

Instructions

Using the downloadable template:

  1. Choose a real work question that needs information.
  2. Write the information need in one clear sentence.
  3. List at least three places you could search.
  4. Write three search terms or phrases, including one alternative term.
  5. Search in at least two places and record the results.
  6. Apply useful filters, such as date, file type, author, source or category.
  7. Compare the results for relevance, currency and authority.
  8. Record the best source and explain why it is the best fit.

Reflection

Consider:

  • Which search location gave the best results?
  • Which search term or filter helped most?
  • What would you change next time?

Stretch activity (optional)

For a deeper challenge:

  • Repeat the search using an approved AI-supported tool, then verify its suggestions in authoritative systems.
  • Create a short search tip for your team based on what you learned.

Evidence of completion

You have completed this activity when:

  • The information need is clear.
  • At least two search locations and three search terms are recorded.
  • The most authoritative result is identified and explained.

1.2 Evaluating information

What it is

Assessing the credibility, reliability, accuracy and relevance of information, data and digital content. This includes critically interpreting information, identifying trustworthy sources, recognising misinformation and disinformation, understanding potential bias, and judging whether information is fit for purpose.

Good evaluation also means checking whether information is current, evidence-based, relevant to the task and appropriate for the decision being made.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

"Can I trust and use this information?"

rather than simply:

"Can I find this information?"

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Queensland Government employees regularly use information to develop policy, provide advice, make decisions, manage programs and deliver services.

Not all information is equally reliable. Information may be:

  • Outdated
  • Inaccurate
  • Incomplete
  • Biased
  • Misleading
  • Generated by AI
  • Lacking evidence or context

Strong evaluation skills help employees:

  • Provide evidence-based advice
  • Make informed decisions
  • Reduce operational and reputational risks
  • Maintain public trust
  • Identify misinformation and unreliable content
  • Use information responsibly and ethically
  • Support reliable briefings, reports and service decisions
  • Assess AI-generated content critically before relying on it

As digital information and AI-generated content become increasingly common, the ability to critically evaluate information is becoming an essential workplace capability.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Compare multiple sources before preparing a briefing
  • Check whether data used in a report is current and accurate
  • Verify information produced by an AI tool before using it
  • Assess whether a website is an authoritative source
  • Evaluate information provided by a stakeholder or supplier
  • Review evidence before making recommendations
  • Identify bias or conflicting viewpoints across sources
  • Check whether information aligns with current legislation, policy or standards
  • Determine whether a source can be relied upon for decision-making
  • Distinguish facts from opinions, assumptions or interpretations
  • Review a briefing note or policy advice against current evidence and legislative requirements
  • Check whether an AI-generated summary reflects the source material before sharing it
Tools and processes

Tool availability and configuration differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the digital systems and search environments used to find, assess and use information, data and digital content.

  • ForGov
  • Queensland Government websites
  • Australian Government websites
  • Queensland legislation websites
  • Microsoft Copilot and approved AI tools, used to assist investigation rather than as authoritative sources
  • Search engines
  • Microsoft Learn
  • SharePoint and internal knowledge repositories
  • Research databases
  • Open data portals
  • Reporting and analytics platforms

Common processes

Processes are the evaluation strategies and habits that help you judge whether information is reliable and fit for purpose.

  • Relevance and fitness for purpose: Does this source answer the question or support the decision being made?
  • Authority and provenance: Who created it, who published it, and is the source credible and accountable?
  • Currency and status: Is it current, updated, and still relevant to the context?
  • Evidence and accuracy: Is the information supported by data, references, or verifiable evidence?
  • Purpose and possible bias: Why was it produced, and could the source be shaped by a particular agenda or viewpoint?
  • Corroboration: Does the same claim appear in other reliable sources?
  • Jurisdiction and context: Is it applicable to Queensland Government, the relevant legislation, and the operating context?
  • Consequence of error: If the information is wrong, how significant would the impact be on the decision, service or advice?

After completing these checks, decide whether the source is:

  • Suitable
  • Suitable with caution
  • Not suitable

Hint: A source can still be useful even when it is not perfect. The key question is whether it is fit for the decision you need to make.

Try it at work
  1. Choose a work topic or decision you are currently working on.
  2. Identify one source you are considering using or sharing.
  3. Determine who created or published the source.
  4. Check the author or organisation’s authority, credibility and accountability.
  5. Review when the source was published or last updated.
  6. Assess whether the source is current and still relevant to your context.
  7. Identify the intended purpose of the source and who it was written for.
  8. Look for supporting evidence, references, data or source material.
  9. Compare the source with at least one other credible source.
  10. Look for bias, assumptions, omissions, conflicting claims or limitations.
  11. Decide whether the information is fit for purpose and consider the consequences of using it incorrectly.
  12. Rate the source as Suitable, Suitable with caution or Not suitable, and explain how you will use it.

Hint: Reliable information is not always perfect information. Good evaluators understand the strengths and limitations of a source and explain how those limitations affect its use.

DigComp 3.0 describes eight proficiency levels grouped into four broad bands. For simplicity, this Hub presents the four bands: Basic, Intermediate, Advanced and Highly advanced. The descriptions below are practical workplace interpretations of DigComp 3.0 and are not verbatim reproductions of the official competence statements.

Basic

With guidance as needed, individuals can:

  • Recognise that digital information may vary in quality and reliability.
  • Identify basic indicators of trustworthy information.
  • Recognise that some online information may be misleading or inaccurate.

Intermediate

  • Assess the credibility and reliability of information sources.

  • Identify indicators of bias or misinformation.
  • Evaluate information before using or sharing it.
  • Recognise the limitations of AI-generated content.

Advanced

  • Critically evaluate information from multiple and complex sources.
  • Assess evidence, credibility and relevance in different contexts.
  • Explain the strengths and weaknesses of information sources.
  • Support others to evaluate information effectively.
  • Apply critical thinking to AI-generated and digital content.

Highly advanced

  • Lead and promote evidence-based information practices.
  • Design or improve approaches for evaluating information quality.
  • Support others to identify misinformation and disinformation.
  • Contribute to organisational approaches for assessing information reliability.
  • Guide complex decision-making using critically evaluated information.
Suggested next practice

Source Credibility Challenge

Objective

Check whether information is credible, current, supported by evidence and suitable for a real work purpose.

Learning outcomes

After completing this activity, you will be able to:

  • Check whether a source is credible and trustworthy.
  • Compare information across multiple sources.
  • Identify bias, gaps, limitations and missing context.
  • Decide whether information is suitable, suitable with caution or not suitable for a work purpose.
  • Explain how the strengths and limitations of a source affect how it should be used.

Instructions

Using the downloadable template:

  1. Choose a current work topic or decision.
  2. Select three sources about the topic.
  3. Record who created each source and when it was published or last updated.
  4. Check the author, organisation or publisher and assess their authority and accountability.
  5. Review the evidence, references, data or source material used.
  6. Identify any bias, assumptions, missing context or limitations.
  7. Compare the main claims across the three sources.
  8. Rate each source as suitable, suitable with caution or not suitable.
  9. Choose the strongest source and explain how it should be used.

Reflection

Consider:

  • What made one source more trustworthy than the others?
  • Did the sources agree or conflict?
  • Which evidence was strongest and why?
  • What information is still missing?
  • How would you explain your decision to a colleague or manager?

Stretch activity (optional)

For a deeper challenge:

  • Ask a colleague to rate the same sources and compare your reasoning.
  • Assess an AI-generated answer against the same checks and record where verification was needed.
  • Compare a current source with a less recent source to see how timing and evidence affect your judgement.

Evidence of completion

You have completed this activity when:

  • Three sources are assessed.
  • Strengths, limitations and any bias or gaps are recorded.
  • A fit-for-purpose decision is made and explained.
  • You can explain how the source should be used, or not used, in your work.

1.3 Managing information

What it is

Organising, storing and retrieving information and data in digital environments. This includes collecting, processing and analysing information and data in structured digital environments so it remains reliable, discoverable, usable and fit for purpose.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

"Can the right people find, understand and use this information when they need it?"

rather than simply:

"Where did I save that file?"

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Government information is both a working asset and evidence of business activity.

Good information management supports accountability, service continuity, lawful recordkeeping, collaboration and informed decision-making. It helps employees find authoritative information, understand which version to use and reduce unnecessary duplication.

Strong information management skills help employees:

  • Locate information quickly when it is needed
  • Maintain reliable and accurate records
  • Reduce duplication and rework
  • Improve collaboration across teams
  • Support compliance with recordkeeping obligations
  • Improve knowledge sharing and organisational memory
  • Enable information to be reused and understood over time

Information must be managed in accordance with applicable recordkeeping, privacy, security, access and disposal requirements. Queensland Government guidance highlights records and information management responsibilities, including recordkeeping, information privacy, right to information, and accessing, publishing and using data.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Organise project documents within a SharePoint library
  • Agree file naming and metadata conventions for a team
  • Apply document properties, permissions and version control
  • Identify the authoritative version of a document
  • Manage documents shared through Teams, SharePoint or OneDrive
  • Organise meeting notes in OneNote
  • Capture key decisions in approved recordkeeping systems
  • Use metadata, filters and search to locate information
  • Prepare and organise structured data in Excel
  • Clean and organise datasets before reporting or analysis
  • Review information repositories to reduce duplication
Tools and processes

Tool availability and configuration differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the digital systems or applications used to store, organise, retrieve, process or analyse information.

Examples include:

  • SharePoint document libraries
  • Teams workspaces
  • OneDrive
  • OneNote
  • Outlook
  • Approved records and document management systems
  • Excel tables and structured datasets
  • Power Query
  • Power BI
  • Metadata and content management tools

Common processes

Processes are the agreed ways of working that help information remain reliable, secure, discoverable and usable over time.

Examples include:

  • Applying file naming conventions
  • Using metadata and document properties
  • Managing permissions and access
  • Maintaining version control
  • Identifying authoritative records
  • Applying retention and disposal requirements
  • Conducting information reviews
  • Maintaining data quality
  • Organising information structures consistently
  • Improving the findability of information

Metadata can help users organise information consistently and locate content more effectively. Examples may include project names, business areas, years, owners, document types and status classifications.

Try it at work
  1. Choose an active Teams, SharePoint or approved records workspace.
  2. Identify one type of information your team regularly creates or receives.
  3. Review how the information is currently named, stored and organised.
  4. Assess whether files can be located easily using search, navigation or metadata.
  5. Identify any duplication, inconsistent naming or missing metadata.
  6. Develop a simple naming convention.
  7. Identify two or three metadata fields that would improve findability.
  8. Ask a colleague to locate the information without being told exactly where it is stored.
  9. Record what made the information easier or harder to find.
  10. Identify one practical improvement your team could make.

Hint: Good information management is not about creating more folders. It is about making information easier to find, understand, access and reuse when needed.

DigComp 3.0-aligned proficiency guide

DigComp 3.0 describes eight proficiency levels grouped into four broad bands. For simplicity, this Hub presents the four bands: Basic, Intermediate, Advanced and Highly advanced. The descriptions below are practical workplace interpretations of DigComp 3.0 and are not verbatim reproductions of the official competence statements.

Basic

With guidance as needed, individuals can:

  • Acknowledge the benefits of managing and organising information in digital environments.
  • Recognise functions of data removal, restoration and backup, and the main properties of digital files and folders.
  • Download, save, retrieve, move and delete digital files.
  • Organise and format simple data in a structured digital environment, such as spreadsheets.
  • Update contacts, such as in email systems or digital directories.

Intermediate

Individuals can:

  • Acknowledge the importance of careful and ethical management of data and information in digital environments.
  • Apply naming conventions to digital files and folder hierarchies.
  • Organise folders, and manage, save and delete files on digital devices, external storage and cloud services.
  • Identify common types of data and their formats, and use data collection tools for simple processing of data.
  • Manage information in digital accounts such as email.
  • Organise and format data and apply basic formulas in structured digital environments such as spreadsheets.

Advanced

Individuals can:

  • Prioritise ethical and transparent management and processing of data and information in digital environments.
  • Apply a variety of functions to transfer and manage data and information in digital environments.
  • Describe examples, applications and limitations of open data and big data.
  • Use a range of digital tools and methods to collect and process a variety of data and information.
  • Apply appropriate analysis to information and data in digital environments to contribute to complex decision-making.
  • Assist others with data and information management, processing and analysis in digital environments.

Highly advanced

Individuals can:

  • Acknowledge the importance of structuring and documenting data and information in digital environments for the benefit of others.
  • Develop and implement strategies for complex or specialised data and information management, processing and analysis in digital environments.
  • Use a variety of digital tools and methods to process, manage or analyse complex data or large volumes of information.
  • Lead or contribute to initiatives that support others in advanced information and data management, processing and analysis in digital environments.
  • Contribute to improvements in or new solutions for data management, processing or analysis in digital environments.
Suggested next practice

Team Information Organisation Review

Objective

Improve how a team workspace stores, names and describes information so people can find and use it.

Learning outcomes

After completing this activity, you will be able to:

  • Review how information is organised.
  • Identify duplication and findability problems.
  • Create a simple naming and metadata approach.
  • Test whether another person can find information.

Instructions

Using the downloadable template:

  1. Choose an active SharePoint, Teams or approved records workspace.
  2. Select one common type of information in the workspace.
  3. Review how the information is named, stored and organised.
  4. Check for duplicates, unclear versions, missing metadata or inconsistent names.
  5. Write a simple naming convention with one example.
  6. Choose two or three metadata fields that would improve findability.
  7. Ask a colleague to find one item without being told its exact location.
  8. Record one practical improvement, an owner and a target date.

Reflection

Consider:

  • What made information easy or hard to find?
  • Could the authoritative version be identified?
  • How will the improvement be maintained?

Stretch activity (optional)

For a deeper challenge:

  • Review a second information type and test whether the same convention works.
  • Draft a one-page team information guide.

Evidence of completion

You have completed this activity when:

  • The current organisation of information is reviewed.
  • A naming and metadata approach is proposed.
  • A findability test and improvement action are recorded.
