5. Problem identification and solving

Build the practical skills needed to identify digital needs, solve problems, improve processes, adapt technologies and continue developing capability in a rapidly changing digital environment.

Official DigComp 3.0 area description

To identify and assess needs, and to use digital technologies and adapt digital environments to meet these needs. To identify and resolve technical and conceptual problems and problem situations in digital environments. To use digital technologies to make improvements in, or new solutions for, processes and products. To build capabilities to operate autonomously in digital environments. To stay informed about digital technological developments and their implications.

Why this area matters

Queensland Government employees regularly encounter technical issues, process challenges, changing technologies and opportunities to improve services.

Strong capability in this area helps employees solve problems efficiently, make better use of digital tools, adapt to change, improve service delivery and continue building capability over time.

Using workplace tools

Tool availability and approval differ between agencies.

Employees should use approved technologies and follow applicable agency requirements, information management obligations, cyber security requirements and technology governance processes.

5.1 Identifying and solving technical problems

What it is

What it is

Identifying technical problems when using devices, software, systems or digital environments, then applying appropriate steps to resolve or escalate them. This includes recognising common issues, testing simple fixes, using help resources and knowing when to seek support.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

"What is the problem, what have I already tried, and what is the next sensible step?"

rather than simply:

"Why isn't this working?"

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Queensland Government employees rely on digital tools, devices and systems to deliver services, communicate, manage information and complete day-to-day work.

Even small technical problems can delay work, reduce confidence, interrupt service delivery or create unnecessary pressure on support teams.

Strong technical problem-solving skills help employees:

  • Resolve simple issues safely and efficiently
  • Reduce avoidable delays and interruptions
  • Explain problems clearly when seeking support
  • Use approved help channels appropriately
  • Avoid risky workarounds
  • Maintain confidence when digital tools do not work as expected
  • Support colleagues with common digital issues
  • Improve productivity and service continuity

This does not mean every employee needs to become technical support. It means building enough confidence to recognise common problems, try safe first steps and escalate effectively when needed.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Diagnose a recurring issue affecting a business process and gather evidence before escalating it for support.
  • Compare multiple potential solutions to determine the safest and most effective response to a technical problem.
  • Document a successful resolution and share it with colleagues to reduce future disruptions.
Tools and processes

Tool availability and configuration differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the systems, applications or support resources used to identify, test or resolve technical issues.

Examples include:

  • Agency service desk or ICT support portal
  • Microsoft 365 help and troubleshooting resources
  • Windows settings and update notifications
  • Microsoft Teams device and meeting settings
  • OneDrive sync status
  • Browser settings and cache controls
  • Approved knowledge bases and how-to guides
  • Device restart and update functions
  • Agency system status pages where available
  • Approved remote support tools
  • Approved AI tools for explaining error messages or troubleshooting steps, where authorised

Common processes

Processes are the safe, structured steps used to diagnose, resolve or escalate technical problems.

Examples include:

  • Identifying what is not working
  • Checking whether the issue affects only you or others
  • Restarting the device or application where appropriate
  • Checking connection, permissions, settings or file location
  • Repeating the action carefully to confirm the issue
  • Following approved troubleshooting guidance
  • Recording the error message, time and affected system
  • Avoiding unapproved workarounds
  • Escalating issues through approved support channels
  • Sharing the resolution with others where useful
Apply this competency at work
  • Think of a recent technical issue you experienced.
  • Write down what was not working.
  • Identify which area the problem related to: device, software, connection, file location, permissions, account access or settings.
  • Record what you tried first.
  • Check whether approved help guidance was available.
  • Identify what information would help a service desk understand the issue.
  • Record the final resolution, if known.
  • Identify one thing you would do differently next time.

Hint: Good troubleshooting is not guessing until something works. It is working through the issue safely, step by step, and knowing when to escalate.

DigComp 3.0-aligned proficiency guide

DigComp 3.0 describes eight proficiency levels grouped into four broad bands. For simplicity, this Hub presents the four bands: Basic, Intermediate, Advanced and Highly advanced. The descriptions below are practical workplace interpretations of DigComp 3.0 and are not verbatim reproductions of the official competence statements.

Basic

With guidance as needed, individuals can:

  • Recognise that technical problems are common in digital environments.
  • Identify common technical problems with devices and digital environments.
  • Recognise that technical problems can have different causes.
  • Follow instructions to resolve common issues, such as lost connectivity, forgotten passwords, lost file locations, unsaved documents or incorrect email or web addresses.
  • Recognise when to seek help to resolve a technical problem.

Intermediate

Individuals can:

  • Select appropriate strategies to resolve technical problems.
  • Adapt troubleshooting strategies based on the context.
  • Use search, help resources or digital tools to troubleshoot technical issues.
  • Update or adjust settings on common devices and peripherals to maintain performance.
  • Build confidence and autonomy in resolving common technical problems.

Advanced

Individuals can:

  • Evaluate whether a technical solution has worked.
  • Resolve more complex technical problems in digital environments.
  • Decide when a problem requires specialist support.
  • Support others to identify and resolve common technical issues.
  • Apply troubleshooting approaches across different tools, systems or work contexts.

Highly advanced

Individuals can:

  • Guide others in solving complex technical problems.
  • Contribute to improved troubleshooting processes or support resources.
  • Identify patterns in recurring technical issues.
  • Help improve digital environments so common problems are reduced.
  • Support teams to build confidence in safe and effective problem-solving.
Test your capability

Technical Troubleshooting Log

Objective

Use a safe, step-by-step approach to understand, test and record a technical problem.

Learning outcomes

  • Describe a technical issue clearly.
  • Use logical troubleshooting steps.
  • Gather useful information for support.
  • Record the resolution for future use.

Instructions

Using the downloadable template:

  1. Choose a recent or common technical issue.
  2. Describe what was expected and what happened instead.
  3. Record the tool, device, time, error message and people affected.
  4. Classify the likely area and list safe steps tried.
  5. Check an approved help resource.
  6. Record when and how to escalate.
  7. Document the resolution or information needed in a support request.

Reflection

Consider:

  • Which step gave the best clue?
  • When should troubleshooting stop?
  • What could help next time?

Stretch activity (optional)

Compare several logs to identify a recurring pattern and create an “if this happens again” guide.

Evidence of completion

  • The issue and evidence are recorded.
  • Safe troubleshooting steps are documented.
  • The resolution or escalation information is clear.

5.2 Identifying needs and digital technological responses

What it is

Identifying needs and evaluating, selecting and using digital technologies or possible digital solutions to meet those needs. This includes adapting digital environments to suit personal, team or accessibility needs, and making informed choices about which tool or approach best fits the task.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

"What do I need to achieve, and which digital option best supports that need?"

rather than simply:

"Which tool do I already know how to use?"

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Queensland Government employees work across many different roles, services, systems and digital environments. Choosing the right digital tool or response can improve efficiency, accessibility, collaboration, customer service and decision-making.

Strong capability in this area helps employees:

  • Match digital tools to real work needs
  • Avoid unnecessary duplication or overcomplicated solutions
  • Use approved tools more effectively
  • Recognise when accessibility or workplace adjustments are needed
  • Adapt digital settings to support different ways of working
  • Select tools that are secure, usable and fit for purpose
  • Communicate business needs more clearly to ICT, data or service design teams
  • Support more inclusive and user-centred digital services

Queensland Government's Digital Service Standard requires agencies to understand user needs, understand the tools and systems required, make digital services secure, build consistent and responsive design, make digital services accessible and test services. These principles are directly relevant when selecting or adapting digital technologies to meet a user or business need.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Assess whether a business challenge requires a process change, a technology solution or a combination of both.
  • Identify accessibility needs and configure digital tools to support effective participation.
  • Evaluate available technologies against user needs, risks, costs and organisational requirements before recommending adoption.
Tools and processes

Tool availability and configuration differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the digital technologies, features or platforms that may be selected, configured or adapted to meet a need.

Examples include:

  • Microsoft Teams
  • Outlook
  • SharePoint
  • OneDrive
  • OneNote
  • Microsoft Forms
  • Excel
  • Power BI
  • Power Automate
  • Power Apps
  • Microsoft 365 accessibility features
  • Captions, transcripts, dictation, screen readers and magnification tools
  • Approved AI tools
  • Agency service desk or ICT request systems
  • Approved business systems and workflow tools

Common processes

Processes are the steps used to identify the need, compare options and select or adapt the most appropriate digital response.

Examples include:

  • Defining the problem before choosing a tool
  • Identifying who needs to use the solution
  • Checking accessibility, inclusion and usability needs
  • Comparing available approved tools
  • Considering privacy, security and recordkeeping requirements
  • Testing the tool or setting with real users
  • Reusing existing platforms or templates where possible
  • Seeking advice from ICT, accessibility, privacy or information management teams
  • Adjusting settings to support personal or team needs
  • Reviewing whether the chosen digital response solved the original problem
Apply this competency at work
  • Choose one work task, process or interaction that could be improved.
  • Write down the actual need or problem in plain language.
  • Identify who is affected by the need.
  • List two or three approved digital options that could help.
  • Compare the options against purpose, ease of use, accessibility, privacy, security and effort.
  • Choose the option that best fits the need.
  • Test the option on a small scale.
  • Ask one user or colleague whether the option helped.
  • Record whether the tool should be used, adapted or reconsidered.

Hint: Good digital problem-solving starts with the need, not the tool. The best solution may be a simple setting change, a better use of an existing tool, or a non-digital step that supports people who cannot use the digital option easily.

Test your capability

Digital Needs and Tool Fit Review

Objective

Compare approved digital options and choose the response that best meets a real user or business need.

Learning outcomes

  • Define the need before choosing a tool.
  • Compare options using clear criteria.
  • Consider accessibility, privacy and security.
  • Test whether the selected response works.

Instructions

Using the downloadable template:

  1. Choose one work need, process or user problem and write it clearly.
  2. Identify who is affected and what they need to achieve.
  3. List at least three approved tools, settings or non-digital options.
  4. Compare fit, usability, accessibility, privacy, security, effort and reuse.
  5. Choose the best option and explain why.
  6. Test it with one user or small group.
  7. Record feedback and decide whether to keep, adapt, escalate or stop.

Reflection

Consider:

  • Did you start with the need?
  • Which criterion changed the decision?
  • Did the test show that the need was met?

Stretch activity (optional)

Use a weighted scoring method and test the preferred option with users who have different access needs.

Evidence of completion

  • The need and users are clear.
  • Options are compared consistently.
  • Testing and a final decision are recorded.

5.3 Identifying creative solutions using digital technologies

What it is

Identifying ways digital technologies can be used creatively to solve problems, respond to opportunities, improve services or develop new approaches. This includes recognising the potential and limits of digital technologies and AI systems, and understanding when human judgement, creativity or intervention is still needed.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

"How could digital technologies help us solve this problem in a better or different way?"

rather than simply:

"What tool can I use?"

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Queensland Government employees often work with complex problems, changing community expectations, resource constraints, legacy processes and emerging technologies.

Creative use of digital technologies can help teams:

  • Improve services and processes
  • Reduce manual effort and duplication
  • Generate new ideas or options
  • Prototype and test solutions before investing heavily
  • Use data and evidence in new ways
  • Improve accessibility and inclusion
  • Support more connected, responsive and efficient government services
  • Identify where AI, automation or digital tools may help, and where they may not

Queensland Government's Digital Economy Strategy identifies digitally enabled government as a priority, including using digital technologies to transform how government operates, manages data and invests in technology.

Creative digital problem-solving does not mean adopting new technology for its own sake. It means starting with a real need, exploring options, testing ideas safely and choosing approaches that create practical value.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Use data, digital tools and stakeholder insights to redesign a process that improves service outcomes.
  • Test innovative approaches to reduce manual effort while maintaining governance and quality requirements.
  • Work with colleagues to develop a digital solution to a recurring operational problem.
Tools and processes

Tool availability and configuration differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the digital technologies that can help explore, prototype, test or deliver creative solutions.

Examples include:

  • Microsoft Whiteboard
  • Microsoft Forms
  • Microsoft Teams
  • SharePoint
  • Excel
  • Power BI
  • Power Automate
  • Power Apps
  • Microsoft Copilot or other approved AI tools
  • Miro, Figma or other approved design tools
  • Digital prototypes or mock-up tools
  • Data dashboards and reporting tools
  • Approved collaboration and ideation platforms

Common processes

Processes are the creative and structured ways of working that help teams identify, test and improve digital solutions.

Examples include:

  • Defining the problem before choosing a solution
  • Understanding user needs and pain points
  • Mapping the current process
  • Exploring multiple solution options
  • Using design thinking or human-centred design approaches
  • Prototyping a small-scale solution
  • Testing with users or colleagues
  • Assessing risks, benefits and limitations
  • Considering privacy, security, accessibility and sustainability
  • Checking whether an existing government solution can be reused
  • Reviewing whether technology is the right response
  • Iterating based on feedback
Apply this competency at work
  • Choose one small work problem, pain point or opportunity.
  • Write the problem in one sentence.
  • Identify who is affected and what they need.
  • List three possible ways digital technologies could help.
  • Include at least one low-cost or no-cost option using an approved tool you already have access to.
  • Identify the benefits, risks and limitations of each option.
  • Choose one option to test or prototype on a small scale.
  • Ask one or two users or colleagues for feedback.
  • Decide whether the idea should be kept, improved, stopped or escalated.

Hint: Creative digital solutions do not have to be big or expensive. A useful improvement might be a better form, clearer dashboard, simpler workflow, improved template or smarter use of an existing tool.

Digital Solution Sketch

Objective

Explore several digital responses to a real problem and sketch one low-risk idea for testing.

Learning outcomes

  • Define a problem and user need.

5.4 Identifying and addressing digital capability needs

What it is

Understanding where your own digital capability needs to be improved or updated, seeking opportunities to develop, and keeping up to date as digital technologies, tools and ways of working evolve. This also includes supporting others to develop their digital competence where appropriate.

In practical terms, this means being able to answer:

"What digital skills do I need to strengthen next, and how will I keep improving?"

rather than simply:

"Can I use the tools I need today?"

Why it matters in Queensland Government

Digital capability is not a one-off skill set. Tools, systems, cyber risks, AI capability, data practices and service expectations continue to change.

Queensland Government's Digital Capability Framework supports employees, leaders and organisations to keep pace with evolving technologies, improve service delivery and drive innovation.

Strong capability in this area helps employees:

  • Recognise their own digital strengths and development needs
  • Build confidence with changing technologies
  • Prioritise learning that is relevant to their role
  • Seek help, guidance or training when needed
  • Stay informed about emerging tools, risks and expectations
  • Support colleagues to build confidence
  • Contribute to a more digitally capable and adaptable public sector

The Australian Government recognises DigComp as a preferred framework for digital skills and training activities, reinforcing the value of using a common framework to identify and improve digital capability.

Workplace scenarios

You may be demonstrating this competency when you:

  • Reflect on capability gaps identified through a project and create a targeted development plan.
  • Seek learning opportunities to build confidence in emerging technologies such as AI, automation or data analysis.
  • Coach colleagues in digital practices and help them identify relevant learning pathways.
Tools and processes

Tool availability and configuration differ between agencies. Use only systems approved by your agency.

Common tools

Tools are the digital systems, resources or platforms that can help identify, develop or track digital capability.

Examples include:

  • Digital capability self-assessment tools
  • DigComp 3.0 capability framework resources
  • Queensland Government Digital Capability Framework
  • Microsoft Learn
  • Agency learning management systems
  • Communities of practice
  • Capability development plans
  • Manager discussion guides
  • Microsoft 365 help and learning resources
  • Peer learning, mentoring or coaching channels
  • Approved AI tools for learning support, where authorised

Common processes

Processes are the reflective and practical steps used to identify capability needs and continue developing over time.

Examples include:

  • Reflecting on current digital strengths and gaps
  • Comparing capability against role needs
  • Prioritising one or two development areas
  • Setting small, practical learning goals
  • Choosing learning resources that match the need
  • Applying learning in real work tasks
  • Seeking feedback from colleagues or managers
  • Sharing useful resources with others
  • Reassessing progress after practice
  • Keeping up to date with digital changes, risks and opportunities
Apply this competency at work
  • Choose one digital task, tool or capability area that is important in your role.
  • Rate your current confidence as low, medium or high.
  • Identify one thing you can already do well.
  • Identify one thing you find difficult or avoid.
  • Find one relevant learning resource, guide or colleague who can help.
  • Set one small learning goal that can be completed in the next two weeks.
  • Apply the skill in a real work task.
  • Ask for feedback or reflect on what improved.
  • Record what you want to learn next.

Hint: A digital capability gap is not a failure. It is a signal about where focused learning or practice could make work easier, safer or more effective.

  6. Practise the skill in a real work task.
  7. Ask for feedback or compare the result with your starting point.
  8. Record what improved and choose the next learning step.

Reflection

Consider:

  • What evidence shows improvement?
  • Which learning method worked best?
  • What should you practise next?

Stretch activity (optional)

Plan two or three connected skills over six months and support a colleague while comparing learning approaches.

Evidence of completion

  • A capability gap and goal are documented.
  • Learning is applied in real work.
  • Evidence, reflection and a next step are recorded.
